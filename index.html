How it works

An industrial bill is four separate charges wearing one total. The decoder rebuilds each one from your figures, then rebuilds the whole bill a second time with the recommendations applied. The difference between those two complete bills is the headline figure — computing it that way is the only way to avoid counting the same rupee twice, because the three levers interact.

The demand charge, and its two traps

Demand is billed on the higher of your recorded maximum demand and a minimum percentage of your contract demand. That produces a trap in each direction, and most factories are caught by one of them.

Drawing more than you contracted. The part of your MD above contract demand is billed at a multiple of the normal rate — commonly twice, sometimes more. The decoder splits that into the part you would have paid anyway and the penal premium, which is excess × rate × (multiple − 1). Only the premium is avoidable money; the rest is electricity you actually used.

Contracting more than you draw. If your MD never reaches the minimum-billing floor, you pay the floor regardless. That is max(0, contract × minimum% − recorded MD) × rate every month, for capacity that was never delivered to you. The tool recommends a revised contract demand covering your worst demand on record plus your chosen headroom, rounded up to the step your licensee accepts, and never below that peak — a saving bought by guaranteeing next year's penalty is not a saving.

The 12-month peak matters more than this month's. One low month will talk you into a contract that penalises you in the busy season, so the recommendation is driven by the highest MD you enter for the year, and the tool reports when raising the contract to cover a seasonal spike costs more in minimum billing than the penalty it removes.

Power factor, and sizing the capacitor

Power factor is cos(φ): the share of the apparent power you draw that does real work. Below the target in your tariff you pay a surcharge for every 0.01 you fall short; above it you earn a rebate. Where demand is billed in kVA, a poor power factor costs you twice, because kVA = kW / PF — the same machines register a bigger demand.

The capacitor bank that closes the gap is sized by the reactive power it has to supply:

kVAR = kW × ( tan(acos(PF_now)) − tan(acos(PF_target)) )

At 100 kW moving from 0.80 to 0.95 that is 100 × (0.75 − 0.3287) = 42.13 kVAR. The kW is the real load behind your recorded peak, which is recorded MD × recorded PF on a kVA-billed tariff; enter your own figure if you know it. Multiply by your installer's rate per kVAR, add the panel, and the payback is that cost over the monthly saving the correction produces — the surcharge you stop paying, plus the demand charge you stop paying on kVA you no longer draw.

A power factor of zero or above 1 is refused rather than computed. That is not fussiness: acos of anything above 1 is undefined, and tan(acos(0)) evaluates in binary floating point to about 1.6 × 1016 — a finite, plausible-looking number that would size you a bank sixteen quadrillion kVAR large. A target at or below your current power factor is reported as needing no capacitor at all.

Time-of-Day

Units are entered against the off-peak, normal and peak slabs, with the slab rates either as percentages of your base rate or as the outright rates printed in your tariff. Shifting load out of peak saves the rate difference on the units moved: units × (peak rate − destination rate). You cannot shift more than the peak slab actually holds, so the figure is capped at your peak consumption, and if the destination slab is not cheaper the tool says the shift is worth nothing rather than dressing a loss up as a saving.

Why this tool ships no tariff table

Industrial tariffs are determined state by state. Under section 62 of the Electricity Act 2003 each State Electricity Regulatory Commission sets the tariff for its licensees, on terms it specifies under section 61, and it does so roughly every year. The demand rate, the penal multiple, the minimum-billing percentage, the power-factor target and the Time-of-Day slabs are clauses of your distribution licensee's tariff schedule and its general terms and conditions of supply — reissued with every tariff order — and your contract demand is a figure in your own agreement for supply.

A table of state rates shipped inside a tool goes stale within a year and then produces confident wrong rupees on a real bill, which is worse than producing nothing. So every rate, slab, multiple, percentage and target here is yours to enter. The numbers the page opens with are round placeholders chosen to make a worked example legible, and they belong to no state.

Assumptions & rates

Every figure below is an indicative placeholder, not a tariff. They are listed here only so you know exactly what the page is doing before you replace them. Read your own values off your bill and your DISCOM's current tariff order.

Demand. Billed in kVA. Contract demand 1,000 kVA; recorded MD 1,150 kVA; 12-month peak 1,180 kVA; demand rate ₹400 per kVA per month; penal multiple 2× on the excess; minimum billable demand 75% of contract; revision step 5 kVA; headroom 10%; one-time revision cost ₹0.

Power factor. Recorded 0.84; target 0.95; surcharge 1% per 0.01 below target; rebate 0.5% per 0.01 above; no cap; whole 0.01 steps; applied to demand + energy charges; capacitor ₹600 per kVAR installed plus ₹25,000 for panel and installation; sizing kW derived from recorded MD × recorded PF.

Energy and Time-of-Day. Base rate ₹8.00 per kWh; peak +20%; off-peak −10%; 60,000 off-peak units, 190,000 normal, 90,000 peak; 30,000 units assumed shiftable out of peak into off-peak.

Other lines. Fuel / power-purchase adjustment ₹0.50 per kWh on all units; electricity duty 9%, applied to the sum of all charges above it; other charges ₹5,000.

Rules the tool applies to your figures. Billing demand is max(min(MD, contract), contract × minimum%) at the normal rate, plus max(0, MD − contract) at the penal multiple. Power factor must lie in (0, 1]; outside that the power-factor lines are refused and the rest of the bill still decodes. A penal multiple below 1 is read as 1, a minimum-billing percentage outside 0–100 is bounded, negative rates and units are read as zero, and duty is never charged on a negative subtotal. Blank fields count as zero. Where a ratio has no denominator — utilisation with no contract demand, a rate per unit with no units — the tool prints a dash instead of a number.

What is a model, not a reading. Correcting power factor is assumed to shrink the recorded kVA peak in the ratio PF_now / PF_target at constant real power, and the 12-month peak is assumed to improve in the same ratio. Real plant varies: the peak may occur at a different power factor from the monthly average, and an automatic bank that is switching correctly at full load may not be at light load. On a kW-billed tariff, correction changes no demand charge at all and the tool shows no reduction.

This is a decoder and a planning aid, not a bill and not advice. It arithmetic-checks the figures you type against the rules you enter; it cannot know your tariff, and it does not read your bill. Before acting on any number here, confirm the rate, the penal multiple, the minimum-billing percentage and the power-factor clause against your distribution licensee's current tariff order, and confirm your contract demand against your agreement for supply. Revising contract demand is a formal application with its own charges, notice period and limits on how often it can be done; capacitor sizing for an actual installation needs a load study, harmonic assessment and a licensed contractor, because an over-compensated bank at light load causes its own problems. Everything is computed in this page and nothing is sent anywhere.
